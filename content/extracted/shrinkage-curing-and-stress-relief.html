Shrinkage, curing, and stress relief sound like laboratory words. In a house they are usually why a slab or wall opened a line in year one and then stopped talking about it.

This page explains the sequence, why control joints exist, and why “it cracked after we moved in” is not automatically a soil story.

What curing actually is

Hydration is the chemical reaction that turns cement and water into hardened paste. Curing is the practice of keeping that reaction going — moisture and temperature held long enough for the paste to gain strength. If the slab dries too fast at the surface, the top wants to shrink while the interior is still plastic. That mismatch is a common source of early cracks.

What shrinkage is doing

As free water leaves the mix, volume drops. Restraint from the subgrade, the edges, or embedded steel turns that volume change into tension. The concrete relieves the tension by cracking.

This can be designed for. Control joints are planned cracks. Random cracks are often the same physics without the planned location.

Why the first year is noisy

  • Most drying shrinkage shows up early
  • Heat, wind, and a dry climate speed surface drying
  • Interior finishes go on while the structure is still adjusting

A crack that opened at month three and looks the same at month eighteen is often finished work, not a live failure.

What this does not automatically mean

  • The pour was defective
  • The soil collapsed
  • Reinforcing steel failed
  • Repair is required to stop shrinkage

When shrinkage is the wrong explanation

  • The crack is offset, not just open
  • It keeps widening after the first seasons
  • It matches a pattern of movement elsewhere in the house

A final note

This page is educational, not diagnostic. Shrinkage is not a defect by default. It is one of the ways concrete finishes becoming a solid.
