A design calculation predicts what a micropile should be able to carry. A load test is how an engineer checks whether the pile that actually got built can carry it.

This page explains the two real categories of micropile testing, and what the results actually show.

Two different tests, two different questions

A verification test happens early, often on a pile installed specifically for testing rather than one that will carry building load. The question it answers: does the proposed design and installation method actually produce the capacity the design assumed?

A proof test happens later, on selected production piles — the ones actually supporting the structure. The question it answers: does this specific installed pile meet the criteria it was supposed to meet?

These aren't interchangeable steps. Verification checks whether the plan works. Proof checks whether a particular pile was built to that plan.

What a test actually measures

A load test produces a load-movement curve — how much the pile moves at each increment of applied load. An engineer looks at whether movement stabilizes during a hold period, how much movement remains after the load is removed, and whether the pile reaches the required test load without exceeding acceptable movement. Compression, tension, and lateral loading are tested separately, because a pile's behavior under one direction of load doesn't guarantee the same behavior under another.

Common misconceptions

  • "Every micropile gets tested to failure." Usually not. Most projects test selected elements to a specified level; failure-level testing is typically reserved for verification piles, not the ones holding up the building.
  • "A successful test proves every other pile is identical." It increases confidence in the method. It doesn't replace the need for installation records and specified proof testing on the piles actually in the ground.
  • "A compression test proves tension capacity." Not necessarily — the load path and ground response can differ by direction, which is exactly why they're tested separately.

How MFRC suggests homeowners think about it

Instead of asking "was it tested," ask "was it verified as a method, and proven on the piles that are actually carrying the building?" Those are two different, necessary questions — and neither one alone answers the other.

A final note

This page is educational, not diagnostic. Design predicts. Testing is what actually confirms — or doesn't — that the prediction held up in the ground.
