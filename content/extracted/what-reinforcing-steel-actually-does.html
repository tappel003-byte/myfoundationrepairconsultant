Reinforcing steel is often described as if it prevented cracks. It does not. It changes how concrete carries tension after a crack has already formed.

This page explains what rebar and post-tension cable are actually for, what they cannot do, and why "there is steel in it" is not a complete answer.

What the steel is doing

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension — it resists being squeezed far better than it resists being pulled apart. Steel is the opposite: strong in tension. Put together, the two make a member that can handle bending, which plain concrete alone cannot do well.

Once concrete cracks under tension — which it does, routinely, even in well-built work — the steel bridges that crack and carries the tension across it. That keeps cracks narrower and more numerous instead of letting one crack open wide. It is a performance role, not a prevention promise.

Post-tensioning is a different tool with the same limit

Post-tension cable works differently — it's tensioned and anchored after the concrete has gained some strength, which compresses the slab and reduces the tension it has to fight in the first place. That can mean fewer cracks, longer spans, or thinner slabs. But a post-tensioned slab is still concrete, still shrinks, still responds to temperature and restraint, and can still crack if those effects outrun the compression working against them.

What homeowners usually hear

  • "It has rebar, so it shouldn't crack."
  • "Post-tension means the slab can't move."
  • "If it cracked, the steel failed."

A reinforced slab can crack and still be doing exactly what it was designed to do. Engineering guidance on crack control treats a certain amount of cracking as normal and acceptable — the goal is keeping cracks narrow and controlled, not eliminating them.

What reinforcing steel does not do

  • It does not stop soil from changing volume
  • It does not replace drainage
  • It does not make a slab independent of the ground it sits on
  • It does not mean every crack is cosmetic

When steel details start to matter

  • A crack is wide, offset, or still growing
  • A post-tension slab shows unusual warping
  • Someone proposes cutting or drilling without knowing where the steel actually is

How MFRC suggests homeowners think about it

Instead of asking "why did the reinforced slab crack," ask "is the crack behaving the way controlled cracking is supposed to — narrow, stable, and not offset?" Reinforcement's job was never to prevent every crack. Its job is to keep the ones that happen small and predictable.

A final note
