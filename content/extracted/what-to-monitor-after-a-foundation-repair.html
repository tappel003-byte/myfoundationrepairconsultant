Most of what this site says about monitoring is about the period before a repair decision — documenting floor slope, or choosing to watch instead of act immediately. Monitoring doesn't stop being useful once a repair is finished. It changes purpose — from deciding whether to act, to confirming the work is doing what it was designed to do.

Why monitoring continues after the work is done

A repair addresses the condition it was designed for. It does not guarantee that no other condition will ever affect the house again, and it does not always eliminate every symptom immediately — some settling of finishes, minor crack changes, or door adjustment can be normal in the months after work is completed as the house settles into its new support. Distinguishing that expected, short-term settling from a sign the repair isn't performing is exactly what post-repair monitoring is for.

What's reasonable to watch

  • Elevation readings at the points that were measured before the repair, taken at the same locations, on a schedule that matches any monitoring requirement in the warranty
  • Whether existing cracks are stable, and whether any new cracking appears in areas that weren't part of the original repair
  • Whether doors and windows that were adjusted continue to operate normally
  • Drainage and grading around the house, since water management is often part of what keeps a repair working over time
  • Any conditions specifically called out in the repair's documentation as something to watch

How this connects to the warranty

Some warranties specify monitoring or inspection requirements as a condition of coverage — see understanding foundation repair warranties. Skipping a required inspection, even one that seems unnecessary because everything looks fine, can affect coverage later. The warranty document, not general habit, should set the actual schedule.

When to raise a concern

A single hairline crack or one sticking door a year after a repair is not automatically evidence of failure — the same caution about over-reading normal material behavior applies here as it does anywhere else on this site; see material behavior. A pattern of renewed elevation change at the same points that were repaired, or new distress specifically at the repaired location, is what's worth raising with the company that did the work.

Keeping the same simple documentation habit used before a repair — dated photos, consistent measurement points, a written record — makes it far easier to tell normal variation from an actual problem, and gives a repair company something concrete to respond to if a concern does come up.
