Once a floor looks uneven, the next useful move is often not a repair bid. It is a record. Slope that has been stable for years and slope that is still changing are different problems. A short, dated record is how you tell them apart.

This page is a homeowner method. It is not a substitute for a surveyed floor plan. It is enough to keep you from making a large decision off a single afternoon's impression.

What you are trying to learn

You are not trying to prove the house is level. Almost no house is. You are trying to answer three questions:

  • Where is the low area?
  • Is that shape the same next season as it is today?
  • Do other signs in the same area change with it?

A simple record that is worth keeping

  • Date every photo. A phone timestamp is enough if it is visible or saved with the file.
  • Stand in the same doorway and take the same wide shot of the room. Repeat it.
  • Photograph door latches, baseboard gaps, and any crack that sits in the same part of the house as the slope.
  • Write one sentence: what you felt underfoot, which corner looked low, and whether anything else in that zone had changed.
  • Repeat at a useful interval — often ninety days, or after a wet season and a dry season — not every weekend.

What not to treat as a measurement

  • A marble, golf ball, or grape rolling across the floor
  • A phone level app used once, in one spot, with no notes
  • A contractor's story about "this much slope always means piers"
  • Comparing your house to a neighbor's house

Those things can start a question. They cannot finish one.

If you want numbers

A relative measurement is more useful than a dramatic one. Pick two points that will still be there in a year — a threshold and a register, a post and a cabinet toe-kick — and measure the difference the same way each time. Write the method down so you do not invent a new one next season.

A floor elevation survey is a different tool. It maps many points on one day. It is useful when you already have a reason to map the house. It is not required to start a homeowner record.

When monitoring is enough

Monitoring is a legitimate next step when the slope may be old, when other signs are quiet, and when nothing is accelerating. It is not avoidance. It is how you find out whether the house is still telling the same story.

Monitoring stops being enough when the shape is changing, when signs cluster in one zone, or when you need an independent read before you sign a repair contract. At that point the record you already kept is what makes the next conversation honest.

A final note

This page is educational, not diagnostic. The point of a record is not to collect proof for a sale. It is to keep a changing house and a finished house from being treated as the same problem.
