Truss uplift is one of the most common reasons a ceiling crack looks like a foundation story and is not.

This page explains the seasonal movement of roof trusses, why the crack often appears where the wall meets the ceiling, and how to tell it apart from ongoing settlement.

What truss uplift is

A roof truss is a triangle of wood. The bottom chord lives in conditioned space. The top chords live in an attic that gets hot, cold, and dry. When the bottom chord dries and shortens relative to the top, the truss can arch slightly and lift at interior partitions that are not part of the exterior load path.

The finish at that partition records the lift as a crack or a gap that opens in winter and often closes when humidity returns.

What it usually looks like

  • A crack or gap where an interior wall meets the ceiling
  • Tape or paint that splits in heating season
  • Little or no matching distress at the foundation
  • A pattern that repeats year after year in the same place

What it does not automatically mean

  • The roof is failing
  • The foundation dropped
  • The wall is no longer bearing load
  • The house needs piers

Seasonal lift at a partition is a framing and moisture story until something else in the house is changing with it.

When it is the wrong explanation

  • The gap grows and does not recover
  • Exterior walls, floors, or openings are moving in the same season
  • The crack continues through masonry or a foundation element

Related dictionary terms

  • Moisture Content
  • Cosmetic Crack
  • Hairline Crack
  • Settlement
  • Load Path
  • Differential Settlement

(Each of these terms is explained in the MFRC Foundation Dictionary.)

A final note

This page is educational, not diagnostic. A ceiling crack that breathes with the seasons is often the truss, not the soil. Time and a dated photo tell you which.
