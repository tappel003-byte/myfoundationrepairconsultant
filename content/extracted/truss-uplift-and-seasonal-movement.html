Truss uplift is one of the most common reasons a ceiling crack looks like a foundation story and is not.

This page explains the seasonal movement of roof trusses, why the crack often appears where an interior wall meets the ceiling, and how to tell it apart from ongoing settlement.

What truss uplift actually is

A roof truss is built from wood members oriented in different directions, sitting in two very different environments at once. The bottom chord lives just above the ceiling, in conditioned space, often buried in insulation. The top chords sit up in the attic, exposed to whatever the attic air is doing — cold and dry in winter, hot in summer.

Wood changes dimension with its moisture content, especially across the grain. When the top chords dry out more than the insulated bottom chord during heating season, the truss can bow or arch very slightly as the two parts respond to different conditions. That small shape change lifts the truss a little where it crosses interior partition walls — walls that are framed from the floor below and don't move with it.

Why it shows up at interior walls specifically

The drywall connection at that wall-to-ceiling corner has to absorb whatever relative movement happens between the truss above and the partition below. Exterior walls are usually built into the same structural system as the truss, so they move together; an interior partition is not, so it gets left behind. That's the corner where the crack shows up.

What it usually looks like

  • A crack or gap where an interior wall meets the ceiling
  • Tape or paint that splits specifically in heating season
  • Little or no matching distress at the foundation
  • A pattern that returns, in the same place, year after year

What it does not automatically mean

  • The roof is failing
  • The foundation dropped
  • The wall is no longer carrying load
  • The house needs piers

Seasonal lift at a partition is a framing and moisture story until something else in the house is changing with it.

When it is the wrong explanation

  • The gap grows and does not recover in warmer months
  • Exterior walls, floors, or openings are moving in the same season
  • The crack continues through masonry or a foundation element

How MFRC suggests homeowners think about it

Instead of asking "why is my ceiling cracking," ask "does this close up again when the seasons change?" A crack that breathes with the year is usually the truss talking, not the soil. One that only ever gets worse is a different conversation.
