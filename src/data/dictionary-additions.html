Auger-Cast Pile

Also called a CFA pile. A hollow-stem auger is drilled into the ground, then grout or concrete is pumped through the stem as the auger is withdrawn.

Auger-cast piles exist as a deep foundation option. They are not a typical residential repair tool. This work is most often seen on larger commercial or infrastructure projects, not house foundations.

Bond Strength

The resistance along a micropile shaft where grout meets soil and where grout meets reinforcing steel. Capacity comes from that bond along the shaft, not from the pile tip.

Bond strength tends to matter most when someone quotes a micropile capacity. Design usually neglects end bearing and relies on documented soil along the shaft — which is why a capacity number with no soil information behind it is hard to trust.

Caliche

A cemented desert layer of soil and carbonate that can feel like rock to a probe. It is common in parts of Arizona and the Southwest.

Caliche tends to matter most when it stops a probe or drill before the soil that actually governs settlement is reached. Hitting a hard layer is not the same as reaching competent bearing material for the intended load.

Compaction Grouting

A ground-improvement method that injects stiff, low-mobility grout to displace and densify suitable soil. It is not foam and not a conventional deep pile.

Compaction grouting tends to matter most when the identified problem is loose ground, fill, or a void that can be treated in place. It does not create a new load path the way a helical pier, push pier, or micropile does.

Drilled Pier

A large-diameter hole drilled into the ground and filled with reinforced concrete, sometimes with a widened base. Drilled piers exist as a deep foundation option.

They are not a typical residential repair tool. If a bid proposes one on a house, it is worth asking why a smaller underpinning system was not compared first.

Driven Pile

A precast concrete, steel, or timber pile advanced into the ground by impact or vibration rather than drilling. Driven H-piles and I-beams are in this family, though the word I-beam is often used loosely.

Driven piles are not a typical residential repair tool. If one is proposed on a house, it is worth asking whether it is a true load-bearing pile or a steel member only nicknamed an I-beam.

Fill

Soil or other material brought in and placed to raise, level, or build up a site, rather than soil that formed there naturally. Fill can be engineered and compacted in controlled lifts, or placed with little control.

Fill tends to matter most when it was not properly compacted. Uncontrolled fill can settle unevenly under a foundation for years. Fill is not automatically a problem — the question is how it was placed, not just that it exists.

Floor Elevation Survey

A measurement of floor height at multiple points across a structure, used to map how level the floor is and to identify areas of differential movement.

A floor elevation survey tends to matter most as a baseline. One survey is a snapshot. Repeat surveys show whether movement is active or has stabilized. A sales sketch is not the same thing as a mapped survey.

Foam Jacking

The common name for lifting or supporting concrete using expanding polyurethane foam, injected in controlled stages beneath a slab or footing. It is used to fill voids, restore contact, and lift settled concrete.

Foam jacking tends to matter most when the problem is a void or loss of contact under a slab, not a foundation that needs a new deep load path. It is not a pile.

Geotechnical Investigation

A site-specific study of soil, rock, and groundwater conditions, usually involving drilling, sampling, and laboratory testing. The result is information about bearing capacity, moisture behavior, and how the ground is likely to respond to load.

Geotechnical investigation tends to matter most when a repair method, pier depth, or capacity claim depends on what is below the surface. Visual cracks alone cannot replace it.

Helical Pier Capacity

The load a helical pier is expected to carry after installation. Capacity comes from plate bearing and shaft resistance in the soils the pier actually reaches — not from the fact that a pier was installed.

Helical pier capacity tends to matter most when an installation report treats torque or depth as proof. Those readings are useful. They are not a substitute for understanding the soil the plates are in.

Limited Access

A site condition where low clearance, tight setbacks, interior placement, or nearby structures restrict which equipment can reach the work area or operate there.

Limited access tends to matter most when it is used to explain why one method was proposed over another. It is a legitimate reason to choose smaller equipment or a different pier type. It is not, by itself, a reason to skip a geotechnical investigation.

Related:MicropileHelical PierGeotechnical Investigation
Load Testing

A field procedure that applies a known load to a pier, pile, or foundation element and measures how it moves. The test checks performance under load instead of estimating capacity from installation resistance alone.

Load testing tends to matter most on high-value structures, unusual soils, or installations where torque or driving records are inconsistent. Many residential projects never receive a full load test — which is why the assumptions behind a capacity claim should be clear.

Native Soil

Soil that formed in place over time and was not imported as fill. It sits beneath or alongside any fill that may have been added later.

Native soil tends to matter most when comparing it to nearby fill, since the two can behave very differently under the same load. A soil report is usually what distinguishes one from the other, not appearance alone.

Polyurethane Injection

A two-component expanding resin injected into soil or beneath a slab. Near the surface it fills voids and lifts concrete. Injected deeper, it has also been used to improve loose or poorly compacted soil.

The material is the same whether the job is described as foam jacking or deeper soil improvement — what changes is depth, purpose, and documentation. It does not create the same kind of load path as a helical pier, push pier, or micropile.

Torque Correlation

An empirical relationship between the torque used to install a helical pier and an estimated axial capacity. The correlation is useful when soils and helix geometry resemble the conditions used to develop it.

Torque correlation tends to matter most during installation, when it is treated as a live capacity check. It measures rotational resistance, not long-term settlement, lateral strength, or soil layers the pier only briefly passed through.
